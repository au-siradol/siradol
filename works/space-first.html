Sources and limits

About this study: Classification and reference span ranges are drawn from Heino Engel’s Structure Systems. Geometry, supports, preliminary dimensions, loads and comparison scores are selected or adapted by this tool’s developer. These schematic models do not reproduce the book’s illustrations exactly and are not verified structural designs.

Reading the results: Engel supplies the classification and reference span charts. The modeled geometry, preliminary sizing, weights and comparison scores are this tool’s assumptions or adaptations; ≈ marks estimates. Each result names its basis below.

Coverage: the genealogy includes all five families (pp.44–55). The comparison models selected options in families 1–4 and one hybrid. Slabs (3.4) are not modeled yet, including short-span applications; height-active systems (5) are listed for reference, outside this single-storey comparison. “Modeled options” is not the number of systems in Engel’s book.

Book: Heino Engel, Structure Systems, Hatje Cantz, 5th edition, 2013, ISBN 978-3-7757-1876-9. Classification: pp.44–55; span charts: pp.62, 138, 176, 216. Page numbers are printed book pages (PDF page = printed page + 2 in the supplied scan). Chart values are historical reference intervals, not a capacity calculation. Some chart rows cover several variants; transferred ranges are identified per option. Lower ends below this tool’s 3 m input limit are clipped in the database.

Author assumptions: depths, rises, sags, support spacing and own weights are preliminary estimates. Thomas Leslie’s ARCH 445 lectures inform selected models where named. Engel p.200 illustrates two equal-section crossing beams under a point load: at length ratio 1:2, the shorter beam takes 8/9. This tool’s extension to whole grids and space trusses, and its depth multiplier √(2α), are author heuristics, not a general design rule from Engel.

Loads and forces: roof load combines estimated self-weight and covering, services (usually 0.10 kN/m²), and the existing live-load assumptions attributed to Thai Ministerial Regulation No.6 (B.E.2527): 0.30 kN/m², or 1.0 for the concrete-roof cases in this model. These assumptions are separate from Engel and are not a check of current regulatory compliance. Individual force estimates name their statics model and sources. Wind, asymmetric loading, buckling, connection design and prestress losses are not checked. Prestressed concrete span references do not make the displayed dimensions a prestress design.

Measurements: clear-section ratio = L×H divided by the modeled enclosing section area, not usable floor area or volume. Overall footprint width is a transverse distance, not a site area. Interior support counts use modeled support locations inside 0<x<L and 0<y<W; boundary supports and continuous walls are described separately. Visible line density is projected visible structural line length per floor area, not a validated measure of visual calmness.

Lite and Full: Lite keeps Engel’s reading — mechanism, form and space, design potential: no estimated loads, force figures, roof coverings, timber products, ceiling measures or weighted scores; a material shows its span ranges but does not exclude a system; thrust is given in words, H/V being the horizontal force over the vertical load at the same support (thresholds 0.3 / 0.8 are the author’s). Full shows everything.

Building types and shaped spaces: sizes follow Neufert, Architects’ Data, 4th ed., where it gives them — sports hall 27 × 45 × 7 m, the triple multi-functional hall (p.350); workshop sheds 3–6 m high, more with an overhead crane (p.265); a hangar sized for a B747-400, 64.9 m span, 70.7 m long, 19.6 m to the tail (p.423); museum at least 4 m clear (p.208); swimming pool 25 × 16.66 m with 2–4.5 m round it and at least 4.0 m clear over the water (pp.362–365); lecture theatre with 0.90 m rows and 2.30 m clear over the back rows (pp.198–201) — and are the author’s elsewhere (market hall / station concourse, exhibition hall, assembly hall); a seating bowl stops at one 30-row block (p.319): by Neufert’s sightlines more rows, or an upper tier, would rise past 45°; the starting requirements are the author’s. The requirements “daylight”, “hung loads” and “extendable” are the author’s readings of each system; “low” means a total height of at most 1.5 × the clear height. Depths of beams, frames, trusses, Vierendeel girders, steel space trusses and concrete grids follow the span/depth ratios for preliminary design of the Metric Handbook, 3rd ed., ch. 36 Structure — the middle of each range: beams Tables VII, XVIII, XXXVI, XLIV; frames XX, XXXIV, XLVI (single-storey frames: steel L/d 35–40, glulam 30–50, precast concrete 22–30, the knee twice the rafter); trusses XVIII, XLV, XLVIII; space frames XLV; waffle and skew grids XXXIII. Each enlarged drawing names its table and page under Depth. Where the Handbook has no row (other materials, curved and cantilever forms), the ratio is the author’s. Engel gives the forms, not depth ratios. Hybrid systems follow Engel pp.320–327: not a family of their own but two systems linked by superposition, coupling or both; all fifteen of Engel’s are modeled; their depths, sags, mast heights and spans are the author’s first estimates, as Engel gives no span chart for hybrids. Straight-edged hypars follow Engel pp.257–265: umbrellas on centre columns, units on buttresses, conoids with window strips and four hypars on four points are fitted to the hall (unit sizes, rises and edge heights are the author’s reading); the other compositions of pp.260–263 are drawn for reference only — the author’s reading of Engel’s spatial forms, sized to the hall but not fitted to it — and never enter Compare or the suggestions. Folded-plate beams (pp.53, 221): end diaphragms and edge beams h/6 deep are the author’s sizes. Linear shells follow Engel pp.242–244, “folded cylindrical surfaces”: troughs side by side, each a half-cylinder open outward, its depth half its width (the author’s reading of his drawings), folded at the knees and narrowing at the hinges; shell girders stand on end columns (p.53) or cantilever from central supports (p.244).The indoor stadium is a seating bowl round a basketball court (FIBA Venue Guide: 28 × 15 m court in a 2.0 m boundary area, at least 7.0 m free height); the theatre is a raked auditorium with a separate stage house (commonly 1.5–2.5 × the proscenium opening high; 2 × used). The rows follow Neufert, Architects’ Data, 4th ed.: in a sports hall up to 10 rows rise linearly by 0.28–0.32 m a row (0.30 used) and more rows rise parabolically by sightlines — eye 1.25 m, 0.15 m above the eye in front, rows 0.80–0.85 m (0.85 used; FIBA asks for at least 0.865 m), origin 0.5 m over the boundary line (p.354); in a theatre the sightlines run to the O-point at the stage edge at forestage height (0.9 m used), eye 1.10 m, 12 cm every second row with staggered seats (6 cm a row used), rows 0.90 m, and a floor steeper than 10° is stepped (pp.211–212). A step higher than 50 cm needs a guard at least 0.9 m high (p.319); the front barrier is 1.0 m (p.354). Seats come in blocks of at most 30 rows with a cross-aisle of at least 1.20 m behind and between them (p.319); the rows behind an aisle start their sightlines 1.20 m further out. The museum follows Neufert pp.207–208: at least 4 m clear, no direct daylight on the works — light from north-facing or filtered rooflights — and flexible lighting hung from the roof; 20 × 40 × 6 m is the author’s, twice the 10 m gallery Neufert shows (p.207, figs 6–7). The sections draw the seating as a stepped concrete slab (about 0.25 m, the author’s) with a few sightlines. Author assumptions: 3.0 m clear above every row, 2.0 m behind the last row; first theatre row 3.0 m from the stage edge. Each system is raised until its modeled underside clears this space (from its 3D surfaces where they are plain modules, otherwise from its section); systems whose roof can slope along the hall follow the rake instead (a shear: the least front height and rise), by the author’s reading of which forms can slope. Daylight and hung-load judgements in “Your space” are the author’s, not Engel’s.

Ranking: only matching options enter suggestions and combined scores. Single criteria retain equal values; ties are ordered alphabetically, not by an undisclosed depth preference. Combined scores use average ranks for ties, normalized to 0–1 and weighted 50/30/20 (one criterion: 100%; two: 62.5/37.5%). Displayed score = 100×(1−weighted rank); higher is preferred. Scores are relative to the current matching set and are not engineering performance ratings.
