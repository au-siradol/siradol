The question. The building is a toy’s joint made large. Zoom out from one node to the whole building and ask what the joint had to give up at each scale. The node gives up wood. The splice lets in a little steel. The lattice shares its work with concrete floors and a steel stair.

Node. Cidori is a toy from Hida Takayama. Its notched sticks lock when one of them is turned, with no nail or metal. Here the stick is 60×60 mm and 2 m or 4 m long. The assembly sheet (組立図) gives the order: the post, a beam, a second beam, then a turn (回転). The first part of the slider plays it. In the cut drawn here, the post and the first beam are halved over each other. Both are bored by one round hole, with a narrow slot from one corner of the node into it. The second beam has a round neck with two flats. With the flats lined up with the slot, it slides in through the corner. A quarter turn sets its full width across the slot, so it cannot come out. The slot runs on past the corner, so the post and beam each lose a 15 mm triangle of wood next to the node. Every stick passes its load through what is left of its section, wood pressing on wood. The order, the stick and the round cut are from the published sheets (組立図, 部材仕口部 分解図). The exact cut is my reading. The overlap check samples every stick on a 2 mm grid at 41 steps of the assembly. The detail is positioned at a node in the reconstructed third-floor lattice; its exact location in the built structure is unverified.

Splice. The lattice is 9 m high and over 20 m long, so sticks are joined end to end. The joint sheet draws this splice (継手) between two nodes. The ends butt, and a keyaki key (やとい材) 440 mm long lies in a slot across the butt, fixed by four galvanized drift pins Ø10×60. Pushed, the splice needs nothing: wood bears on wood. Pulled or bent, the butt opens, and only the key and pins keep it together. The same splice joins the hand-made units (ユニット図, 2000×2000×3000 mm). Lengths and pins are from the sheet. The key’s size across the stick is read only roughly. The movements are drawn exaggerated.

Load path. The reconstructed lattice is analysed as a space frame of half-metre segments. Previously stored results are invalid after the geometry revision and are not displayed. Every stick is a 60×60 mm beam that can push, pull, bend both ways and twist. Posts stand pinned on the footing. The lattice rests on the 2F and 3F floors where they reach it, and the ceiling band hangs from the 2F slab. “Slabs tie it” also holds those points and the roof sideways. The colour is how much of its strength a stick uses: its push or pull against what the node can carry (about 12 kN), plus its bending against what the stick can carry (about 0.79 kNm). The frame equations are solved by conjugate gradients (an answer refined step by step until what is left over is a billionth of the load). Use “Re-solve” to calculate a case for this geometry; old stored cases are withheld. The checks: what is left over, that the footing and floors carry exactly the total weight, and that the same solver run on one slice gives the plane-frame answers. Assumed: Japanese cedar E 7,000 N/mm², bending strength 22 N/mm². Loads are the sticks’ own weight (4 kN/m³) and the roof at 2 kN/m², over 500 mm on each top node. Sideways is 0.2 times that weight, the base seismic coefficient in Japan. These are teaching values, not the engineer’s. Results depend on these assumed supports and are not a safety assessment of the real building.

Assumed wood for the node and the brace. Japanese cedar, E about 7,000 N/mm², crushing about 18 N/mm². The braced post is taken as free to turn at each brace. These are teaching estimates, not Jun Sato’s test values.

Model accuracy. This is a reconstruction from published raster plans and a section, not an as-built model. The 60×60 mm timber section is documented by Kengo Kuma & Associates. Building dimensions, floor levels, roof pitch and eaves are scaled estimates. The roof has a high western portion, a sloping eastern portion and a tapered front canopy. Slab thickness, stair riser counts, plate thicknesses and connections remain approximate. The stair beside the tall void is reconstructed with three runs and two turns, returning west to the laboratory. The rear service stair starts on the southern run, turns on the western landing, and arrives on the northern run at the eastern corridor; a narrow well separates the runs. Positions and widths were re-registered to the user-supplied full-resolution 1F–3F plans using the 500 mm grid; exact riser counts and dimensions remain unverified. The lattice is reconstructed by interpolating the three published plan masks at each 500 mm level; the upper void closes approximately where shown in section. These intermediate layers and the void depth are inferred, not measured. Individual plan dots still require verification against construction drawings. No numerical accuracy tolerance has been verified. The principal concrete envelope, the electrical-room return seen in section and the lift shaft are shown. Interior partitions and the basement are omitted. Concrete walls and floors form the solid building portion; individual wall reinforcement and load shares are not established by these architectural drawings. Wall thicknesses and opening heights are illustrative. The timber analysis still uses prescribed supports: adding visible concrete walls does not create a coupled concrete-and-timber analysis. Floor levels are reconstructed as 1F +0.00 m, 2F +3.50 m and 3F +6.50 m by comparison with the section grid; the raster dimension text is not fully legible. Slabs are drawn 250 mm thick, and lift walls 150 mm thick, as model assumptions. The analysis uses this reconstructed timber grid with assumed supports; the separately drawn steel stair is not included in the timber solver; it does not validate the reconstructed roof or stair geometry.
